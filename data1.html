1 What is data?

Data is a collection of facts, figures, or information. Every time you like a post, watch a video, or step outside, you are creating data. When you check the weather, scroll through social media, or see a product recommendation, you are using data.

Data is everywhere — and it shapes nearly every aspect of daily life. But what exactly counts as "data"? Let's look at some examples:

📱 Social Media

The number of likes on a post, the time you spend scrolling, the videos you watch — all of these are data points.

🏃 Fitness Trackers

Your step count, heart rate, and calories burned are collected continuously throughout the day.

🌤️ Weather Forecasts

Temperature, humidity, wind speed, and air pressure from thousands of sensors worldwide.

🛒 Online Shopping

What you search for, what you buy, what you leave in your cart — all used for recommendations.

💡 Key idea: Data is not just numbers. It includes text, images, videos, sounds, and even your location. If it can be recorded, stored, or measured, it can be data.

2 Types of data

Data comes in many forms. Understanding the different types helps us know how to collect, store, and analyze it.

Type Description Examples
Numbers Quantities, measurements, counts Age, temperature, price, test scores
Text Words, sentences, messages Names, comments, addresses, reviews
Images Photos, drawings, screenshots Profile pictures, maps, charts
Audio Sounds, music, speech Voice messages, songs, podcasts
Video Moving images with sound YouTube videos, video calls, movies
Location Where something is or happened GPS coordinates, check-ins, delivery routes

When you use an app, it often collects multiple types of data at once. For example, a fitness app might collect numbers (steps), location (route), and text (workout notes) — all at the same time.

3 How is data collected?

Data collection is the process of gathering information. It happens in many ways, both online and offline. Understanding how data is collected is the first step to becoming a smart digital citizen.

Common ways data is collected

  • Forms and surveys — when you fill in your name, age, or email address online.
  • Cookies — small files websites store in your browser to remember what you did.
  • Sensors — devices like phones, smartwatches, and weather stations measure the world around them.
  • Tracking — apps and websites record which pages you visit, how long you stay, and what you click.
  • Social media interactions — likes, shares, comments, and follows are all recorded.
  • Purchases — every time you buy something online, that transaction is stored.
  • Search engines — every search you type is saved and analyzed.

⚠️ Did you know? Many free apps and websites collect your data to make money. They often sell this data to advertisers, who use it to show you targeted ads. This is why you sometimes see an ad for something you were just talking about!

4 How is data used?

Once data is collected, it can be used in powerful ways. Businesses, governments, scientists, and even your school use data to make decisions.

Real-world uses of data

🎯 Recommendations

Streaming services suggest shows based on what you've watched. Online stores recommend products based on what you've bought.

📈 Improving Products

App developers see which features are used most and which cause problems, then make updates.

🌦️ Predictions

Weather forecasts use past and current data to predict future conditions. Sports teams use data to plan strategies.

🏥 Helping People

Doctors use data to diagnose illnesses. Cities use traffic data to reduce congestion. Schools use data to improve teaching.

Data-driven decisions

When decisions are based on data rather than guesses, they are called data-driven decisions. For example:

  • A school might notice that students who attend tutoring improve their grades — so they expand the program.
  • A city might see that a certain intersection has many accidents — so they install a traffic light.
  • A business might find that customers abandon their carts at checkout — so they simplify the checkout process.

5 Why data literacy matters

Data literacy is the ability to read, understand, question, and work with data. It is an essential skill for the 21st century — just like reading and writing.

When you understand data, you can:

  • Question where data comes from and whether it is trustworthy.
  • Interpret charts, graphs, and statistics correctly — and spot when they mislead.
  • Protect your privacy by understanding what data you are sharing.
  • Make better decisions about what you buy, watch, and believe.
  • Recognize bias — data can be collected or presented in ways that favor one view.

💡 Think about it: If a website says "9 out of 10 people prefer this product," you should ask: Who were the 10 people? How were they chosen? Who paid for the survey? Understanding data means asking good questions.

6 Data privacy and ethics

Because so much data is collected about us, it is important to think about privacy and ethics. Data can be used to help people — but it can also be misused.

Your digital footprint

Every action you take online leaves a digital footprint. This includes posts, comments, likes, searches, and even the websites you visit. Once data is online, it can be very hard to remove.

Questions to ask about data

  • Who is collecting this data?
  • Why are they collecting it?
  • How will it be used?
  • Will it be shared with others?
  • Can I choose not to share it?
  • Is my data safe and secure?

Protecting your data

  • Use strong, unique passwords for each account.
  • Check privacy settings on apps and websites.
  • Be careful what you share publicly.
  • Read privacy policies (or at least skim them).
  • Think before you click — not all links are safe.
  • Remember: if a service is free, you might be paying with your data.

7 Key facts to remember

• Data is a collection of facts, figures, or information.

• Data comes in many forms: numbers, text, images, audio, video, and location.

• Data is collected through forms, cookies, sensors, tracking, and interactions.

• Data is used for recommendations, predictions, improvements, and decisions.

• Data literacy means being able to read, question, and work with data.

• Your digital footprint is the trail of data you leave online.

• Always ask: Who collected this data? Why? How will it be used?

• Protect your privacy: strong passwords, privacy settings, and careful sharing.

• Data can help us — but it can also be misused. Understanding it makes you a smarter digital citizen.
